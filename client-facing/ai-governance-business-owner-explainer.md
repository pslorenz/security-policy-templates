# Using AI Without Giving Away the Business

*A short guide for business owners on where AI creates risk, what an AI governance program does about it, and what is needed from leadership to make it work.*

---

## About this document

This is a client-facing explainer for non-technical business owners and executive sponsors. It pairs with POL-AI-001 (AI and Agent Governance Policy) and TPL-AI-001 (AI Tool and Agent Inventory), which are the operational documents.

The voice uses "we," meaning the organization's security function, IT team, or external security provider speaking to leadership. Leave it in place; it works for either.

Replace [TIMELINE_WEEKS] with a value appropriate to the adopting environment.

## The short version

Your people are likely already using AI. Some of it you bought. Some of it arrived inside software you already pay for, switched on by the vendor. Some of it is a free tool someone found and started pasting business data into.

The risk shows up in three specific places. Company and client data goes into tools that keep it or learn from it. AI tools get connected to email, files, and business systems, with access nobody reviewed. AI agents take actions on someone's behalf, and can be tricked into taking the wrong ones by instructions hidden in an email or a web page.

An AI governance program lets your team keep using AI and closes those three gaps. Banning AI would not work: it pushes the same use onto personal accounts where nobody can see it.

## Why this matters right now

Three things have changed.

First, AI is no longer a separate tool people choose to open. It is built into email, documents, meeting software, and the line-of-business applications you already run, and vendors often turn it on by default. You may already have AI processing your data that nobody here approved.

Second, AI tools have started acting, not just answering. An assistant that can read a mailbox, draft replies, send them, update records, or move files is doing work a person used to do, with that person's access. When it misbehaves, it misbehaves at machine speed.

Third, fraud got better. Voice and video can now be convincingly faked from a few minutes of public recordings. A phone call that sounds exactly like you, asking finance to send an urgent wire, is a real and current threat to businesses your size.

## What we are doing

The program has four parts, sized to your organization.

### One: We are deciding what data can go where

Your data is already classified as Public, Confidential, or Restricted. We are applying the same rules to AI. Public information can go anywhere. Confidential information goes only into AI tools we have approved, under business agreements that say the vendor will not train on it. Restricted information, such as financial account numbers, health details, and passwords, does not go into AI tools at all unless a specific tool has been approved for it.

### Two: We are approving the tools, and finding the ones already in use

We are building a list of every AI tool and AI feature in use, including the ones that came switched on inside existing software. Each one is reviewed before it handles company data: who the vendor is, what they do with the data, whether we can manage access and see what is happening. Approved tools go on a list your team can see. Getting a new tool approved is meant to be quick. The goal is to know what is in use, not to slow anyone down.

### Three: We are controlling what AI can reach and what it can do

Any AI tool connected to company email, files, or systems gets the same treatment as any other account with access: a named owner, only the access it needs, and a quarterly check that it is still needed. Nobody connects an AI tool to company accounts on their own.

For AI agents that take actions, we set limits by how much they do on their own. Some actions always need a person to approve them, no matter how capable the tool is: sending money or changing payment details, emailing people outside the company it has not been approved to contact, changing who has access to what, deleting data, and agreeing to anything on the company's behalf.

### Four: We are keeping people accountable and making fraud harder

The person who uses AI output is responsible for it. Decisions about people, such as hiring, firing, pay, and discipline, are not made by AI alone.

Any request to move money, change bank details, or share credentials gets verified through a separate, known channel, even when the request sounds exactly like you. We will ask you to support this rule publicly, because your staff need to know they will not be in trouble for calling you back to check.

## What we need from you

**Sponsorship.** Someone at the executive level owns this program, approves the policy, and makes the call when convenience and risk pull in different directions.

**A clear message to the team.** People need to hear from leadership that AI use is encouraged within the rules, and that asking for a tool to be approved is welcome. Without that message, people assume the answer is no and stop asking.

**Support for the verification rule.** When finance calls to confirm a payment request that appears to come from you, thank them. That single habit stops most of the fraud this program is designed to stop.

**Decisions on a few judgment calls.** Some uses of AI involve trade-offs that belong to leadership: whether AI can take actions in client-facing workflows, whether meetings with clients can be recorded and summarized, and what you tell clients about your own use of AI.

**Patience on the timeline.** Initial setup takes roughly [TIMELINE_WEEKS] weeks: finding what is in use, approving the tools people need, closing the riskiest connections, and setting up the quarterly review. The urgent items, such as AI tools connected to company accounts without approval, are addressed in the first two weeks.

## What it costs and what it returns

Most of the work is review and configuration, using tools you already have. You may decide to move some teams from free AI tools to business versions, which costs money but is usually the single most effective step, because the business versions come with the data protections the free versions lack.

The returns:

Your team keeps the productivity AI brings, with fewer ways for it to go wrong.

You can answer clients, auditors, and insurers who ask how you control AI, with a policy and an inventory rather than a shrug. Those questions are becoming common.

The fraud that relies on a convincing voice or a lookalike email meets a process that does not care how convincing it is.

## Next steps

Proceed in three stages.

Confirm the executive sponsor and agree on the message to the team.

Run the discovery: find the AI tools, features, and connections already in use, and produce the first inventory.

Review the inventory together, approve the tools your team needs, and agree on the agent limits and the judgment calls above.

From there, the program runs on the quarterly review cadence in the AI and Agent Governance Policy.

Questions about this document or the program can be directed to your security function or service provider.
